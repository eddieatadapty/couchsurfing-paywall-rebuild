The seam

The handoff is the whole point of the rebuild. Today the paywall is generic: it opens on "Couchsurfing Membership" and sells 190+ countries to someone who has one city in mind. After the rebuild the same screen — same rail, same ribbon, same CTA — opens on "Your Paris Membership", sub-lined with the trip window, with the feature row matching the user's first pick promoted to the top and tagged, and the plan pre-selected by trip length rather than by default. Nothing about the screen's construction changed; it just knows five things it didn't know before. That is also what makes the offer paywall land: a second-chance price is a discount when it arrives cold, and a considered concession when it arrives after a personalized payoff.

What I deliberately did not build

  1. A free-vs-paid comparison table. It is the highest-impact pattern in the library and it is the first thing a generic rebuild reaches for. Couchsurfing does have a free tier, but its exact limits are not published anywhere I could verify — and a comparison table with guessed limits is a fabricated claim about what a user does and doesn't get. Ruled out, not forgotten. This is the single biggest upside left on the table — get me the real free-tier limits and it goes in above the plan rail.
  2. A free trial, and any trial timeline. There is no trial SKU in the App Store in-app purchase list, and the app's own categories are Travel and Lifestyle — a vertical where trials measurably hurt LTV rather than help it. Adding one would be both fabricated and, on the category evidence, wrong.
  3. A countdown timer on the main paywall. Timers belong on paywalls that already carry a discount. The main paywall is full price, so a timer there is manufactured urgency. It appears only on the offer paywall, where the 30% is real.
  4. More press logos. The capture's logo strip was mid-scroll, so The New York Times and The Independent are the two I can actually see. There may be others; I won't draw a logo I haven't verified.
  5. Invented testimonials. Both quotes are verbatim 5-star App Store reviews with their real authors.

What I need from you

  1. The real free-tier limits — what a non-member can and can't do (messages? host search? references?). Unlocks the comparison table.
  2. Whether Annual Contribution $13.99 is a live win-back/regional SKU or a legacy price. The offer paywall is built on it being offerable.
  3. Published host counts for the other featured cities (Toronto, Osaka, New York). Only Paris, Bogotá and Dubai carry numbers today, so the rest fall back to the global figure.
  4. Whether Annual Verification $29.99 should appear in this flow at all. It's a real SKU and a recurring complaint in the reviews; I kept it out entirely rather than stack a second ask behind the first.

How to build an onboarding

Most onboarding flows are not bad because someone picked the wrong question. They are bad because the decisions were made in the wrong order.

There is a correct order, and it is short. Pick the shape of the sequence before you pick a single pattern. Then place patterns inside that shape. Then — the step almost everyone skips — run the diagnostic back over your own draft, and treat anything it finds as a defect rather than a suggestion for later.

1  Choose the skeleton→ 2  Place the patterns→ 3  Audit your own draft
1

Pick the skeleton before any pattern

Pattern libraries rank elements, not sequences — and the highest-ranked entry is always the personalization loop, because "no personalized result exists yet" is true by construction of any flow you have not built. Go straight to the pattern list and you produce the same flow every time: goal quiz → loader → "your plan is ready" → paywall. That is a genuinely good flow when the app can back it, and it is the broken-promise failure built deliberately when it cannot. So the shape is a decision with a prerequisite, not a starting position.

SkeletonSpinePick it whenWhat rules it out
Personalization loopgoal quiz → loader → "your plan" → paywall naming the goal The app really uses the answers and you can say what they change Nothing consumes the answers — then the loop is a promise you cannot pay
Value walkthrough3–4 outcome screens → proof → paywall Value is explanatory rather than personalized; no goal data to act on The app genuinely personalizes — the loop beats this every time
Problem → method → proofpain → how it works → proof → paywall The price needs justifying by a method (education, health, finance) Value is visible output — show it instead of explaining it
Commitment-ledgoal → commitment screen → plan → paywall Behaviour-change categories, and the flow is short enough to afford a ritual The flow already asks a lot — another ceremony costs more than it earns
Permission-primed setupvalue → soft prompt → system call → paywall A permission is genuinely required for day-one value The permission is not needed for first value — postpone it past the paywall
Short functionalwelcome → one capability preview → paywall Utilities, where monetization is contextual or limit-triggered The category rewards depth — cutting onboarding has lost on both CR and ARPU

How this one was decided

Chose: personalization loop. The prerequisite is met, and that is the whole reason it was available — Couchsurfing publishes per-city host counts on its own site, so a destination answer looks up a real number rather than needing one invented.

Runner-up: value walkthrough, ruled out precisely because the answers do get consumed. Choosing it would have thrown away data the flow already had.

A missing prerequisite rules a skeleton out; it never licenses a placeholder. Saying which shape you wanted and what you would need to have it is often the most useful line in a teardown, because it tells the team which asset to go get.

2

The eight contradiction rules

These are the diagnostic. They fire on combinations, not on single answers, which is why a checklist never catches them — every individual element can look fine while the combination is the problem. Run all eight before writing a single recommendation.

3

The personalization loop, and where it breaks

The single highest-value structure in onboarding — and most flows implement one or two links and break the chain. The break is almost never the question; it is the missing payoff, or a paywall that never mentions what was captured.

Stage 1
Capture the goal Ask what they want to achieve. Raw material for everything downstream. Here: five questions — destination, trip window, what they want, party, what earns their trust.
→
Stage 2
Loader (optional) Raises perceived value — only if something personalized follows. Otherwise it is pure delay. Here: all five answers named as they are consumed, plus a real review at a captive moment.
→
Stage 3
The payoff Their answer reflected back with something real attached. This is the link that is usually missing. Here: "493,000 hosts in Paris" — a published figure, looked up, not modelled.
→
Stage 4
Paywall that names it The screen that takes the money repeats what onboarding learned. Costs nothing; skipped constantly. Here: title becomes "Your Paris Membership", benefit rows reorder, plan pre-selects.

Echo is honest. Compute is not.

A flow can echo what it was told — the city you chose, the goal you picked — and that is a real, visibly personalized payoff. A flow cannot calculate an outcome nobody measured. "Based on your answers, you'll save 14 hours a month" in front of logic that models nothing is a fabricated proof number wearing a personalization badge, and it ships to real buyers.

The test is simple: could you show the user where that number came from? "Our site publishes it" passes. "It felt about right" does not. Where no real figure exists, echo the answer and drop the number — an absent element is a question; an invented one is a claim.

4

Size the change honestly

The most common failure in a teardown is assigning a restructure-sized number to a copy tweak. The tier is set by the kind of change, not by how important the screen feels.

TierWhat it coversExpected effect
Copy-onlyRewrite a headline, change tone, reword a question, reorder existing linesCR +3–8%
Element add / fixSocial proof screen, progress bar, before/after, plan summary, moving a permissionCR +5–20%
StructuralFlow length, branching, paywall placement, removing a sign-up wall, adding the loopCR +10–20% · ARPU +10–35%
Category mismatchA trial present where trials hurt LTV in that verticalPotentially the largest single lever — frame as a test

Reordering four benefit rows that already exist is copy tier, even when it is the smartest change on the screen. Going from no payoff to a real one is structural. Keeping these apart is what makes the rest of a teardown believable.

5

Trials help in half of all categories, and hurt in the other half

The most counterintuitive thing in the discipline, and the reason "always offer a free trial" is wrong roughly half the time. In several categories direct purchasers are worth materially more than trial users, so a trial actively lowers LTV.

Trials help LTV

Offer one, and put it on the onboarding paywall — the strongest configuration there is.

  • Utilities
  • Health & Fitness
  • Education
  • Photo & Video

Health & Fitness converts best of any category on trial-to-paid — and retains worst, so the flow should set realistic expectations rather than hype.

Trials hurt LTV

Direct purchasers are worth materially more. Do not add a trial reflexively here.

  • Productivity
  • Lifestyle
  • Graphics & Design
  • Entertainment

In one category direct buyers came in around $56.95 against $49.13 for trial users; in another, trial users were roughly 21% less valuable.

How this decided the offer paywall in this build

This app's App Store categories are Travel and Lifestyle — the hurt column. There is also no trial SKU anywhere in its in-app purchase list. So a trial would have been both fabricated and, on the category evidence, the wrong instrument.

The second-chance offer instead uses a real published alternative SKU — a $13.99 annual sitting beside the $19.99 annual, exactly 30% less. The strikethrough is between two prices the store already lists, which is the difference between a discount and an inflated reference price. Roughly 90% of trial starts and 44% of all purchases happen on day zero, which is why the onboarding position matters: the same offer shown after a later in-app paywall has lost badly.

6

Length is the wrong question

There is no target screen count. Subscription onboardings run from three screens to twenty-five, and long quiz-style flows are standard in health, fitness and education. The evidence favours depth: cutting onboarding short lost on both CR and ARPU, roughly 13% each. So short flows should be challenged, not congratulated — but "add more screens" is never the finding on its own.

The real question is payoff density. For each step ask: does this gather something the flow later uses, teach something that justifies the price, or build trust? If none of the three, it is drop-off with no return. A twenty-screen flow that captures goals and delivers a genuine payoff beats a five-screen flow that asks a goal question and drops it.

That is also why naming the weak step is part of the job. In this build, question 4 (travel party) is explicitly flagged as the one to cut first — it survives only because it is consumed twice and is a filter hosts actually use. A flow where every question is defended equally is a flow nobody stress-tested.

7

Run it on your own flow

The same pass, as a checklist. Anything you cannot tick is either a finding or a question for whoever owns the app.

Patterns come from Adapty's teardowns and A/B tests across subscription apps; impact ranges are expected effect, not measured lift for any one app. Figures quoted as measured results (loader removal, sign-up deferral, category LTV, day-zero share) are results from that body of testing, not from this app.

Grounding rule that governs every number on the prototype tab: if it is a price, a rating, a quote or a partnership, it comes from the company's own published material — store listing, site, or the captured screen itself.
